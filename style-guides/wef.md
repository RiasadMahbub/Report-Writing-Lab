# Style Guide — WEF Global Risks Report

## Structure (stable across editions)

```
Preface
Overview of methodology
Key findings
Chapter 1: [near-term risk landscape, e.g. "A world of growing divisions"]
Chapter 2: [long-term/structural risk landscape, e.g. "The point of no return"]
Appendix A: Definitions and Global Risks List
Appendix B: Global Risks Perception Survey (methodology + full results)
Appendix C: Executive Opinion Survey: National Risk Perceptions
Appendix D: Risk governance
Partner Institutes / Acknowledgements
```

## Method (this is the backbone — always name it)

- **Global Risks Perception Survey (GRPS)**: ~900 experts/leaders score dozens
  of named risks on likelihood and severity, over defined time horizons
  (2-year and 10-year).
- Risks are pre-categorized into five domains: economic, environmental,
  geopolitical, societal, technological.
- Risk-interconnection maps are generated from a separate survey question
  asking respondents to name 3–6 most-connected risk pairs.

## Writing patterns

- Chapter titles are evocative, almost headline-style: "A world of growing
  divisions," "The point of no return" — unusual for an institutional report,
  worth noting as a deliberate WEF house-style choice.
- Sentences stack structural nouns as subjects: "The multi-decade structural
  forces... and the interactions they have with each other have continued
  their march onwards."
- Heavy use of named, capitalized risk labels treated as proper nouns
  throughout ("State-based armed conflict," "Societal polarization").
- Numbered rankings are a recurring rhetorical device ("ranked as the number
  one near-term global risk").

## Template

```
[Evocative chapter/section title]

[Structural-forces framing sentence naming 2–4 macro trends.] [How these
trends are interacting / compounding this year.] [Survey-based ranking or
statistic, attributed to the GRPS.] [One sentence on why this matters for
the next 2/10-year horizon.]
```

## What NOT to copy without a real survey behind it

WEF's authority comes entirely from the survey instrument. Do not write in
this style ("ranked as the top risk," "X percent of respondents") unless you
actually have survey data to cite — otherwise it reads as an unsupported
claim dressed in WEF's clothing.
